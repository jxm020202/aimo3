This document is a compilation of a bunch of silly nicknames and terms
that have become popular on the Internet among modern teenagers.

Important disclaimer (how to use this file)
Before writing this handout, even I (Evan) did not know several of the names here. If you
are a beginner or intermediate level geometry student, I do not recommend memorizing
the names or configurations in this file. The reason is:
• If it wasn’t already in EGMO at least implicitly, then it doesn’t come up often
enough to be worth learning for beginners.
• When it does come up, intermediate students can often figure the relevant facts
on-the-spot anyway. Thus, the only real value is documenting the “slang” name for
a fact that they could derive on their own.
Instead, this handout is meant as an encyclopedic reference that you can refer to when
you hear some bizarre name on the Internet that does not appear in the textbook EGMO.

§1 Basic configurations
§1.1 Reim’s theorem
Reim’s theorem is a nickname given for a certain easy angle chasing statement. To fully
state it, we give the following somewhat silly definition.
Definition 1.1. We say two lines `1 , `2 are antiparallel with respect to m1 and m2 , if
the four points `i ∩ mj are the vertices of a cyclic quadrilateral.
Theorem 1.2 (Reim’s theorem)
Fix two lines m1 and m2 . Prove that if `1 is antiparallel to `2 and `2 is antiparallel
to `3 , then lines `1 and `3 are either parallel or coincide.
Proof. Because ](m1 , `1 ) = −](m2 , `2 ) = ](m1 , `3 ).
See Figure 1; I’ve highlighted the angles in green. You’ll notice this is literally a
two-step angle chase, which means that for practical purposes, whenever you see the
name “Reim’s theorem”, it’s just a shorthand for a certain two-step anglechase.
So, if you ever come up to me with the excuse “I didn’t solve the problem because I
didn’t know Reim’s theorem”, I will be sassy in my reply.

§1.6 The first isogonality lemma
The following lemma appears in Geometry Revisited, Section 1.9, Exercise 3. It comes up
once in a blue moon, and has earned the name “isogonality lemma”.
Lemma 1.7 (The first isogonality lemma)
Let ABC be a triangle and let P be a point inside it satisfying ∠ABP = ∠P CA.
Let Q be the reflection of P across the midpoint of BC. Then ∠BAP = ∠CAQ.
Proof. Most straightforward would be to use barycentric coordinates, but you can see a
synthetic proof at https://aops.com/community/c6h518987.
The condition ∠ABP = ∠ACP is actually usually more naturally interpreted as saying
the points B, C, BP ∩ AC and CP ∩ AB are cyclic. Similarly, the parallelogram BP CQ
is noteworthy.

§2.2 Prism lemma
The following simple lemma from projective geometry is useful for transferring cross
ratios. The name is because the picture looks like a pyramid (see Figure 8). I’m told that
the origin story came from me mentioning during the test review of TSTST 2019/9 that
“this motif comes up often, so we should have a name for it”, and one student suggested
“prism lemma”, which stuck.
Lemma 2.2 (Prism lemma)
Two lines `1 and `2 meet at a point D. Let A1 , B1 , C1 be distinct points on `1 and
A2 , B2 , C2 be distinct points on `2 , different from D. Then A1 A2 , B1 B2 , C1 C2 are
concurrent if and only if (A1 B1 ; C1 D) = (A2 B2 ; C2 D).
Proof. This is a restatement of the fact that projection through a point preserves cross
ratios.

§2.3 EFFT (EGMO Theorem 7.16 and 7.25)

two displacement vectors are perpendicular. Teenager Evan, in 2012, thought it was

repository have been largely unsuccessful, and Evan has given up.
