## Documented failure modes of LLMs on competition mathematics

A useful way to think about failure in contest maths is: a solver must succeed at **language-to-maths translation**, **strategy selection**, **multi-step reasoning**, and **final-answer discipline** (format/constraints); the literature shows persistent weak points in each stage, even as raw benchmark accuracy rises. citeturn12view0turn25search14turn25search0

**Problem misinterpretation / misunderstanding requirements.** Large-scale error categorisation work on math word problems identifies “misinterpretation of problem requirements” as a cross-dataset error pattern and notes that ambiguity in the problem statement (not just mathematical weakness) can be a frequent driver of errors. citeturn12view0 Related closer-to-human analyses similarly highlight “unwarranted assumptions” and difficulty translating informal/physical intuition into correct mathematical steps, which is often the same underlying phenomenon as “reading the problem wrong.” citeturn25search0

**Boundary / off-by-one / incomplete constraint handling.** Error pattern studies explicitly call out “incomplete constraint consideration” and the emergence of errors described as failing to analyse boundary conditions; importantly, these are reported even for stronger generations of models, suggesting the problem is not simply “small model arithmetic.” citeturn12view0

**Process–answer inconsistency (compute X, submit Y).** Google’s Minerva analysis gives a concrete example class: the model can compute the correct quantity for an intermediate step but then mistakenly output it as the final answer (a direct analogue of your “computes X but submits Y”). citeturn11search7 The same broad phenomenon is treated as a systematic concern in more recent error-taxonomy work (labelled “process-answer inconsistency” / related issues), underlining that “good-looking reasoning text” is not a reliable indicator of correct final choice. citeturn12view0turn11search12

**Ignoring contradictory evidence / post-hoc rationalisation.** A growing interpretability/safety literature shows that chain-of-thought style explanations can be *unfaithful*—models may produce superficially coherent arguments that rationalise an answer even when it is inconsistent with other answers or evidence, and this can occur without artificial “bias injection.” citeturn11search1turn11search12 Work from entity["company","Anthropic","ai lab"] similarly cautions that you cannot always rely on what “reasoning models” report about their reasoning, which has practical implications for your TIR setting: a model can “sound convinced” and still be wrong, so verification must be externalised into checks/tests. citeturn11search6turn11search12

**Small-case extrapolation failures (pattern from n≤6 breaks at n=9).** This sits inside a broader empirically documented limitation: transformers often struggle with extrapolation to longer/harder instances than those seen in training or in-context exemplars, and length/generalisation performance can be fragile and formatting-dependent. citeturn18search11turn18search0turn18search1 In contest maths terms, “I checked small n and guessed the pattern” is exactly the kind of brittle extrapolation these studies warn about. citeturn18search11turn18search5

**Over-reliance on familiar theorems / standard templates.** Several analyses of LLM mathematical reasoning failures describe a tendency toward pattern-driven approaches (including “over-reliance on numerical patterns”) and difficulty in strategic planning for multi-step deductions. citeturn25search0turn1search1 This is consistent with the view that LLM generalisation often proceeds by mapping a new problem onto familiar structures; when the correct solution requires a less common construction, the “most probable template” bias can mislead. citeturn1search1turn1search9turn18search14

**Timeout spirals / brute-force fixation.** While “timeout spirals” are more a systems behaviour than a single-paper category, the broader agent/tool literature explains why it happens: tool-integrated paradigms expand what a model can do, but without explicit prompting or training signals, models can still get stuck in locally plausible loops (keep trying variations of the same failing plan). citeturn0search2turn21search1turn12view0 This is exactly why competition solutions repeatedly introduce explicit *exploration* and *early stopping / time management* mechanisms rather than trusting a single plan. citeturn16view0turn15view0

## Prompt engineering techniques for maths reasoning with empirical support

Your environment (TIR with Python execution, no internet, 16 parallel attempts, majority vote) is aligned with multiple strands of evidence-backed work; the most valuable prompt engineering is therefore about *steering how the model uses the attempts and the tool*, not about adding more mathematical “knowledge” into the prompt. citeturn21search0turn19search1turn12view0

**Sampling diversity + aggregation (self-consistency).** Self-consistency replaces greedy decoding by sampling multiple diverse reasoning paths and selecting the most consistent answer, producing sizeable gains on arithmetic and reasoning benchmarks (including SVAMP/AQuA). citeturn19search1 Your “16 parallel attempts + majority vote” is an operationalisation of this idea; prompt-level leverage is mainly in (i) ensuring attempts are genuinely diverse (temperature/seed + instruction to explore distinct approaches), and (ii) ensuring each attempt includes *verification hooks* (so “most common wrong answer” is less likely). citeturn19search1turn12view0

**Explicit exploration/backtracking (Tree-of-Thought).** Tree-of-Thought motivates moving beyond left-to-right commitment by exploring multiple “thoughts,” self-evaluating, and backtracking—explicitly targeting the failure mode “early decisions can lock you into a bad path.” citeturn21search1 In practice, you can emulate a lightweight ToT effect inside a single attempt by requiring: “propose 2–3 candidate strategies, score them against constraints/computational feasibility, then pick one,” and across attempts by assigning different strategy styles. citeturn21search1turn12view0

**Problem decomposition (least-to-most prompting).** Least-to-most prompting shows that decomposing a hard problem into a sequence of simpler subproblems can improve generalisation to harder instances than seen in exemplars, including for mathematical reasoning tasks. citeturn19search0 For olympiad-level problems, the prompt analogue is: require the model to produce an explicit “subgoal ladder” (reformulation → lemma search → computation/verification → assembly) before heavy algebra or code. citeturn19search0turn25search14

**Tool-integrated reasoning formats (ToRA; PAL/PoT).** ToRA frames mathematical solving as interleaving natural language rationales and tool calls, and reports meaningful gains versus rationale-only or program-only formats. citeturn21search0 PAL similarly offloads computation to a runtime (Python), improving accuracy by shifting the LLM’s role to *problem decomposition into runnable steps*. citeturn0search25 Program-of-Thoughts further formalises “reasoning as code” for numerical reasoning tasks. citeturn21search11 For your setting, prompt engineering should make the *tool boundary crisp*: the model must treat Python output as the ground truth for numeric subclaims while treating its own prose as a hypothesis generator. citeturn21search0turn0search25turn24view0

**Self-verification / self-checking prompts.** Work explicitly on “self-verification” in mathematical reasoning argues that instructing models to verify can improve reasoning reliability, and other methods (e.g., unit consistency checks) operationalise verification as a structured filter on candidate solutions. citeturn0search15turn0search22 Separately, error-aware prompting research shows that appending relevant error patterns can yield measurable gains, whereas naive “static error examples” can even degrade performance—suggesting verification guidance should be *precise and targeted*, not generic nagging. citeturn12view0

**Does “restate the problem” help?** Direct, clean ablations on “restate in your own words” specifically are rarer than for self-consistency / tool use / decomposition, but recent work on CoT faithfulness argues that models sometimes “recall” important information from the question that is missing in the produced reasoning, and proposes explicitly recalling extra information from the question to enhance reasoning and improve effectiveness/faithfulness. citeturn20view0 Practically, this supports your intuition: forcing an explicit restatement/constraint extraction step is a reasonable, evidence-consistent way to reduce misreadings. citeturn12view0turn20view0

**Does “multiple interpretations” help?** Again, direct ablations are limited, but the combination of (a) misinterpretation being a documented major error class, citeturn12view0 (b) ToT-style exploration explicitly targeting early commitment, citeturn21search1 and (c) self-consistency improvements by sampling diverse paths citeturn19search1 provides a principled basis for an “interpretation branching” prompt: treat different parses as candidate “thoughts,” test via consequences, and only then commit. citeturn21search1turn19search1turn12view0

## Mapping your identified patterns to documented phenomena

Below, each of your observed patterns is mapped to the closest recurring phenomena in research, with implications for prompting that are *more specific than generic “be careful” instructions*. citeturn12view0turn25search0turn25search14

**Ambiguity resolved toward the “most common” sense.** This aligns closely with error categories such as “misinterpretation of problem requirements” and with qualitative reports of “unwarranted assumptions.” citeturn12view0turn25search0 Prompt implication: you want an explicit “disambiguation protocol” (enumerate possible meanings, then look for forced constraints that eliminate interpretations) rather than hoping the model picks the intended meaning. citeturn12view0turn21search1

**Linear constructions chosen when exponential ones exist.** This can be seen as a special case of pattern/template bias: models prefer familiar low-growth analogies unless prompted to consider combinatorial explosion mechanisms (subsets, sign choices, independent binary decisions). The pattern-matching literature is consistent with this: LLMs rely on induction/pattern mechanisms that can be powerful but also brittle under distribution shift or when the surface form suggests the wrong template. citeturn1search9turn18search14turn25search0 Prompt implication: require a “growth rate sanity check” (ask: does the construction naturally yield 2^N objects? how many degrees of freedom exist?), especially in algebra/combinatorics problems involving independent choices. citeturn25search14turn12view0

**Small‑n brute force → wrong extrapolation (pattern changes at n=9).** This is the contest-maths version of length/extrapolation fragility: even when small instances are solved, the inferred rule can fail beyond the explored range. citeturn18search11turn18search0turn18search5 Prompt implication: force the model to treat small‑n patterning as *hypothesis generation*, followed by either (i) proof/invariant derivation, or (ii) targeted search for counterexamples beyond the initial range (within budget). citeturn18search11turn12view0

**Trusting MILP/ILP output without questioning the formulation.** This is a variant of “tool authority” bias: the tool is deterministic, but only with respect to the encoded model, and reasoning agents can make silent modelling mistakes (wrong constraints/objective). Tool-integrated maths work emphasises that the LLM must correctly translate the problem into tool calls for the tool to help. citeturn21search0turn0search25turn24view0 Prompt implication: require a post-solve *witness check* that maps the solver’s output back to the original problem language (verify each constraint in plain terms), rather than trusting feasibility flags alone. citeturn12view0turn21search0

**Non-integer answer submitted for integer-required problem.** This is consistent with “incomplete constraint consideration” errors, where the model’s reasoning path fails to enforce all requirements implied by the statement or expected output type. citeturn12view0turn15view0 Prompt implication: promote “output-domain constraints” (integrality, range, formatting) into the same explicit ledger as mathematical constraints, and force a final constraint audit before emitting the answer. citeturn12view0turn0search15

## Evidence-weighted system prompt rules to add

Each rule below is a single sentence suitable for a system prompt; citations point to the closest supporting evidence (error analyses, prompting studies, or tool-integrated reasoning results).

1. Restate the problem in your own words and extract every constraint (quantifiers, domains, “exactly/at least”, boundary cases, and what the answer must look like) before attempting any solution. citeturn12view0turn20view0  
2. If any wording is ambiguous, enumerate at least two plausible interpretations and run a quick consequence check (logical or computational) to rule out interpretations before committing. citeturn12view0turn21search1turn19search1  
3. Maintain an explicit constraint ledger and consult it whenever you introduce a variable, apply a theorem, or choose a computational model. citeturn12view0turn25search14  
4. Do not assume a “standard template” solution applies until you verify its preconditions against the ledger (and if any precondition is uncertain, treat it as false until proven). citeturn25search0turn12view0  
5. Whenever you infer a pattern from small cases, treat it as a hypothesis and either prove it via an invariant/induction or actively search for a counterexample beyond the initial range. citeturn18search11turn18search0  
6. Before finalising an answer, perform a boundary audit by explicitly checking degenerate/extreme cases and confirming the argument does not silently change at the endpoints. citeturn12view0  
7. When using Python/tool output, reconcile every derived numerical claim with the tool result and never output a final answer that contradicts a validated tool computation. citeturn21search0turn0search25turn11search7  
8. If your reasoning and your computations disagree, assume the *problem interpretation or constraint handling* is wrong until you can localise a concrete bug. citeturn11search7turn12view0turn11search1  
9. After proposing a solution, run a dedicated verification pass that checks the final candidate against all constraints (including integrality/range/format) and rejects it if any constraint is unverified. citeturn12view0turn0search15turn0search22  
10. When you build an optimisation/solver formulation, translate the solver’s output back into a human-readable witness and verify each original constraint directly from that witness. citeturn21search0turn12view0turn24view0  
11. For each attempt, intentionally vary the high-level strategy (e.g., algebraic transformation vs combinatorial invariant vs constructive example vs computational search) to reduce early strategy fixation. citeturn21search1turn19search1  
12. Prefer decomposed solving: write and solve the subproblems in sequence, and only then assemble the final result, rather than trying to solve the whole problem in one uninterrupted chain. citeturn19search0turn25search14  
13. Treat any “convincing” chain-of-thought as a draft and prioritise external checks, because explanations can be post-hoc rationalisations rather than faithful evidence of correctness. citeturn11search1turn11search12turn11search6  
14. Use tool calls to offload exact computation, but keep the tool interaction minimal and purposeful by ensuring each call answers a clearly stated subquestion. citeturn21search0turn0search25turn0search2  
15. If you cannot fully verify a solution within the time/tool budget, explicitly switch to finding a simpler equivalent formulation or a proof sketch that can be independently checked, rather than iterating minor variations of the same approach. citeturn21search1turn12view0turn16view0
