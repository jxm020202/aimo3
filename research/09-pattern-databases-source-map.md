# Pattern databases and technique recognition: a source map

**No single "pattern database for problem solving" exists in mathematics — but partial implementations thrive across chess, medicine, software engineering, and cognitive science.** The closest chess analog combines ~500 ECO opening codes, all ≤7-piece endgame positions (Syzygy), and ~50 tactical motif categories (CT-ART). In software, the Gang of Four's 23 design patterns set the template with explicit "when to use" metadata. In math, the gap is real: after exhaustive searching, **no published resource provides a comprehensive "if you see feature X, try technique Y" decision tree for competition mathematics.** What exists instead are strategy-organized books (Engel, Zeitz), walkthrough-style handouts (Evan Chen), and a frozen wiki of tricks (Gowers' Tricki). Below is a detailed source map of everything found.

---

## Part 1: Pattern databases that already exist

### Chess: three separate databases covering openings, endgames, and tactics

**ECO (Encyclopaedia of Chess Openings)** — Exactly **500 codes** (A00–E99) across 5 volumes, created by Chess Informant (Belgrade, 1974–1979, now in 5th edition). Codes are defined by halfmove sequences from the initial position, at varying depth (1–28 plies). Online at **365chess.com/eco.php**, **chessgames.com/chessecohelp.html**, and Wikipedia's "List of ECO codes." ECO is purely classificatory — **no strategic guidance is embedded**, just annotated move sequences with Chess Informant symbols. A rival system called MICOS claims to classify 3,884 openings across 170 sub-groups, but it's not widely adopted.

**Syzygy Tablebases** (Ronald de Man) cover all endgame positions with **up to 7 pieces** including kings. Queryable free at **syzygy-tables.info** and via the python-chess library. Two file types per endgame: .rtbw files (Win/Draw/Loss with 50-move rule awareness on a 5-valued scale) and .rtbz files (Distance To Zeroing move). Sizes: 5-piece = ~939 MB; 6-piece = ~150 GB; 7-piece = ~18.4 TB. The older Nalimov tablebases cover 3-to-6 pieces (~1.2 TB for 6-piece) with DTM (Depth To Mate) metric but ignore the 50-move rule. The Lomonosov 7-piece tablebases (~140 TB, Moscow State University, 2012) have been **offline since a 2021 ransomware attack**. None of these include "when to apply" guidance — they are raw position-to-result oracles.

**CT-ART (Chess Tactics Art)** by GM Maxim Blokh is the most structured tactical pattern catalogue. CT-ART 4.0 contains **2,200 basic + 1,800 auxiliary exercises across 50 topics** in 17 major tactical categories: annihilation of defense, distraction, decoy, discovered attack, opening of a file, space clearance, X-ray attack, interception, blocking/encirclement, destruction of pawn shelter, pawn promotion, intermediate move, stalemate play, restricting material, pursuit, conjunction of methods, and advanced topics. Available at **chesskingtraining.com**. Each position includes a 5×5 mini-position hint conveying the tactical essence. Chess Tempo (**chesstempo.com/tactical-motifs**) uses **34 community-tagged motif categories**. There is no single canonical list of tactical motifs — sources range from 15–20 fundamental categories to 50+ detailed sub-topics.

**Silman's "How to Reassess Your Chess"** (4th ed., 2010, ~600 pages) provides the **strategic/positional** counterpart: a systematic evaluation framework built around **7 imbalances** (superior minor piece, pawn structure, space, material, key file/square control, weak squares, development lead). This is the rare chess resource with explicit "when to apply" guidance — the entire book teaches a thinking process: assess imbalances → form plan → calculate. De la Maza's "Rapid Chess Improvement" (2002, 128 pages) is a methodology book directing readers to solve ~1,000 tactical puzzles 7 times on an accelerating schedule; **it contains no tactical catalogue of its own**.

### Medicine: illness scripts plus four major diagnostic engines

**Illness scripts** are the theoretical framework for how clinicians store diagnostic patterns, formalized by Henk Schmidt & Remy Rikers. Structure: **enabling conditions** (patient context) → **fault** (pathophysiology) → **consequences** (signs/symptoms) → **management/course**. Key references: Custers (2015), "Thirty years of illness scripts" in *Medical Teacher* 37(5):457–62; Lubarsky et al. (2015) in *Canadian Medical Education Journal* at **pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4795084/**; and structured templates at **clinicalreasoning.org/illness-scripts/**. The enabling conditions serve as the recognition trigger — "elderly diabetic with fever" activates different scripts than "young athlete with chest pain."

**Isabel DDx** (isabelhealthcare.com, now EBSCO) accepts free-text clinical features and uses a proprietary ML/NLP pattern recognition engine against curated documents covering **over 10,000 conditions**. Output: ranked differential with "don't miss" red-flag diagnoses, linked to DynaMed and BMJ Best Practice. Founded 1999 after a near-fatal misdiagnosis. Reports **96% correct diagnosis in top 10**. **UpToDate** (Wolters Kluwer) covers **25+ specialties and 12,000+ clinical topics** authored by 7,600+ physicians, with GRADE-methodology recommendations and algorithmic clinical pathways. **DXplain** (Mass General, 1987) maps **5,000 clinical manifestations to 2,200+ diseases** using modified Bayesian logic with heuristic risk-ranking. **SimulConsult** covers ~2,600 diseases in neurology/genetics using Bayesian inference with temporal reasoning, uniquely ranking suggested next findings by diagnostic utility.

The common architecture across all systems: symptoms/findings → weighted conditions (probabilistic or rule-based) → suggested next tests/actions. All modern systems include "when to apply" in the form of red-flag alerts, next-best-test recommendations, or algorithmic pathways.

### Software engineering: the richest pattern catalogues with explicit "when to use" metadata

**Gang of Four Design Patterns** (Gamma et al., 1994) defined the standard: **23 patterns in 3 categories** (5 Creational, 7 Structural, 11 Behavioral). Each pattern entry contains 13 sections: Pattern Name/Classification, Intent, Also Known As, Motivation, **Applicability** (explicit "when to use" section), Structure (UML), Participants, Collaborations, Consequences, Implementation, Sample Code, Known Uses, Related Patterns. The Applicability section is the recognition trigger — it explicitly describes situations where the pattern applies.

**Enterprise Integration Patterns** (Hohpe & Woolf, 2003) at **enterpriseintegrationpatterns.com** — **65 patterns in 9 categories** (Integration Styles, Messaging Systems, Channels, Construction, Routing, Transformation, Endpoints, System Management). Each entry: problem statement (phrased as a question serving as the recognition trigger), solution statement, sketch diagram, and detailed ~10-page discussion. Pattern icons ("GregorGrams") available under Creative Commons. **Fowler's PoEAA** at **martinfowler.com/eaaCatalog/** adds **~51 patterns in 10 categories**, each with explicit "when to use it" sections. **Azure Cloud Design Patterns** at **learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/patterns/** provide **~40 patterns** mapped to Well-Architected Framework pillars. **Microservices.io/patterns/** (Chris Richardson) catalogs **44 patterns** as an interconnected visual pattern language with problem/forces/solution/context for each.

Software engineering is the domain where "pattern database for problem solving" has been most fully realized — each pattern includes structured metadata, applicability conditions, trade-offs, and related patterns.

### Cognitive science: the theoretical foundation for why pattern databases work

The **~50,000 patterns estimate** for chess mastery originates from Simon & Gilmartin (1973), "A simulation of memory for chess positions," *Cognitive Psychology* 5:29–46. They built a program called MAPP and extrapolated that mastery requires **10,000–100,000 chunks**, with 50,000 as the order-of-magnitude midpoint. The foundational experiment is **Chase & Simon (1973), "Perception in chess," *Cognitive Psychology* 4:55–81** (available on ScienceDirect). Masters reconstructed positions after 5 seconds of viewing but not random positions; the difference is chunking — experts encode boards in larger meaningful units (**3.6 pieces/chunk** for masters vs. 2.7 for weaker players). Crucially, chunks aren't inert — they **trigger associated moves** (the pattern-move theory). This built on **de Groot (1946/1965), *Thought and Choice in Chess*** (JSTOR: jstor.org/stable/j.ctt46n0r2), whose subjects included Alekhine and Euwe. Gobet & Simon later extended chunks into the **template theory** — larger, flexible structures with variable slots.

**Schoenfeld's "Mathematical Problem Solving" (1985, Academic Press, 426 pages)** is the definitive work on expert-novice differences in math. His **four-category framework**: (1) **Resources** — facts and procedures at one's disposal; (2) **Heuristics** — rules of thumb, but Schoenfeld showed Pólya's are too broadly stated to be directly teachable; (3) **Control/Metacognition** — decisions about when and what to deploy (the critical differentiator: novices pursue dead-end approaches for 20+ minutes; experts constantly monitor progress); (4) **Belief Systems** — one's mathematical worldview. The key finding: experts classify problems by **deep structure** (underlying principle/solution method) while novices classify by **surface features** ("it's a triangle problem"). This parallels Chi, Feltovich & Glaser (1981) on physics. Schoenfeld's most-cited paper: "Learning to Think Mathematically" (1992) in Grouws (Ed.), *Handbook of Research on Mathematics Teaching and Learning*, pp. 334–370, reprinted in *Journal of Education* 2016 at journals.sagepub.com. **Schoenfeld's "control" component is exactly the "when to apply" guidance** that's missing from most math resources.

### AI case-based reasoning: the architecture existed but was never seriously applied to math

The **4R cycle** (Retrieve → Reuse → Revise → Retain) is formalized in Aamodt & Plaza (1994), "Case-Based Reasoning: Foundational Issues," *AI Communications* 7:1, available at **iiia.csic.es/~enric/papers/AICom.pdf**. **CYRUS** (Janet Kolodner, 1980, Yale) was the first CBR system ever built — storing diplomatic events of Cyrus Vance in hierarchical Episodic Memory Organization Packets. **CHEF** (Kristian Hammond, 1986, Yale) created new cooking recipes by retrieving and adapting existing ones, with a failure memory guiding when NOT to apply certain patterns. **PRODIGY/ANALOGY** (Veloso & Carbonell, CMU, 1990s) was the most sophisticated — it stored not just solutions but **complete problem-solving traces with justifications at every choice point**, enabling context-sensitive replay. Key paper: Veloso & Carbonell (1993), *Machine Learning* 10:249–278.

**Has CBR been applied to mathematics?** After extensive searching: **no published system specifically stores math solution patterns and retrieves them CBR-style for new problems.** The closest: CBR-PROMATH (Mokmin & Masood, 2015) profiles learners, not problems. PRODIGY/ANALOGY's general traces could theoretically apply to math domains but never were. MathSeer (Rochester Institute of Technology, concluded 2022, **cs.rit.edu/~dprl/mathseer/**) is a math-aware information retrieval system, not a pattern-matching solver. **This is a genuine gap in the CBR literature.**

### Tricki: Gowers' frozen wiki with the right structure but insufficient content

**URL: tricki.org** — a **static snapshot frozen since August 2020**. Conceived by Fields Medalist Timothy Gowers with contributions from Terence Tao, launched April 2009 on Drupal. By September 2010, Gowers wrote "Is the Tricki dead?" (gowers.wordpress.com/2010/09/24/is-the-tricki-dead/) acknowledging low contribution rates — "it was quite hard to make a really good contribution." The site accumulated an estimated **100–200 articles** before going dormant. Dynamic features no longer work.

The structure was exactly right for a pattern database: hierarchical navigation via "What kind of problem am I trying to solve?" (problem-type front pages), subject-area front pages, general problem-solving tips, and tag-based browsing. A typical article includes: general description of a technique, examples with expandable sections, **"how to recognize when to use" guidance** (a core design feature per Gowers' original spec), prerequisites, and related techniques. **Tricki is the closest any math resource has come to an explicit "problem-type → technique → when to apply" database**, but it died from insufficient contributions.

### AoPS Alcumus: adaptive practice with a topic taxonomy, not a pattern database

**URL: artofproblemsolving.com/alcumus** — Free adaptive learning system using **Bayesian updating with an IRT-like model** similar to chess Elo ratings. Covers **7 subjects** (Prealgebra, Algebra, Number Theory, Geometry, Counting & Probability, Intermediate Algebra, Precalculus), each aligned to AoPS textbooks and subdivided into focus topics. Estimated **100–200+ distinct subtopics** total, though no exact public count exists. Subjects map to textbook chapter/section organization.

Internal mechanics (from AoPS blog at **artofproblemsolving.com/blog/articles/alcumus-a-peek-under-the-hood-of-our-adaptive-learning-tool**): every problem has a difficulty score, every student has a per-topic rating (0–100), a logistic probability function matches students to appropriately challenging problems, and a **topic dependency map** determines sequencing. Problems draw from AMC 8/10/12, MATHCOUNTS, AIME, and original AoPS problems, each with detailed solutions linking to textbook readings. The tagging is **subject → topic** hierarchy, not a technique-recognition taxonomy — you can't browse by "technique" (e.g., "try generating functions here"), only by topic area.

## Part 2: Math technique sources with approach guidance

### Yufei Zhao's MIT handouts: terse lecture notes with occasional discovery narrative

**URL: yufeizhao.com/olympiad/** — approximately **13–15 handouts** spanning algebra (~5: Integer Polynomials, Inequalities, Polynomials, Determinants, Linear Algebra), combinatorics (~4: Algebraic Techniques, Bijections, Counting in Two Ways, Tiling), and geometry (~4: Cyclic Quadrilaterals, Three Lemmas, Power of a Point, Similarity). Number theory is underrepresented. Each handout: technique exposition → problem list (~10–18 problems). Written as MOP/camp lecture notes — terse but insightful. The Inequalities handout notably discusses **how to discover solutions** (e.g., "turning the table around" — switching constraint and objective), but this is embedded in exposition, not structured as "when to use" checklists. Zhao recommends starting with the geometry handouts (Cyclic Quadrilaterals, Three Lemmas).

### Evan Chen's ecosystem: the most comprehensive free olympiad resource with walkthrough pedagogy

**OTIS program** (web.evanchen.cc/otis.html) — **70+ distinct units** across algebra (16 units), combinatorics (22 units), functional equations (4 units), geometry (20+ units), and number theory, organized by difficulty: B-level (HM/bronze IMO), D-level (bronze/silver), Z-level (silver/gold). Full catalog viewable at **web.evanchen.cc/static/otis-samples/synopsis.html**. Each unit: walkthroughs (worked examples showing the discovery process) + 15–25 olympiad problems. ~400 students/year.

**OTIS Excerpts** (free, web.evanchen.cc/excerpts.html) — **202 problems and solutions across 13 topics**, covering inequalities, functional equations, combinatorics (global, local, rigid, free), and number theory. No geometry (EGMO covers that). The **walkthroughs are the key pedagogical innovation**: guided question sequences revealing the metacognitive process of solution discovery. Example: for USAMO 2003 inequality, the solution notes "This is a canonical example of tangent line trick" and explains **why the equality case motivates trying that technique**.

**Free handouts** (web.evanchen.cc/olympiad.html) — ~20 PDFs covering: proof-writing, functional equations, Lagrange multipliers, inequalities (SOS method), probabilistic method, barycentric coordinates, complex numbers, directed angles, incenter/excenter lemma, orders modulo a prime, CRT, and more. **The Napkin** (web.evanchen.cc/napkin.html, ~1,000 pages) covers undergraduate-to-graduate mathematics — not an olympiad resource.

**EGMO** (Euclidean Geometry in Mathematical Olympiads, MAA 2016) at **web.evanchen.cc/geombook.html** — 11 chapters in 3 parts: Part I Fundamentals (angle chasing, circles, lengths/ratios, configurations), Part II Analytic (computational geometry, complex numbers, barycentric coordinates), Part III Advanced (inversion, projective geometry, complete quadrilaterals, personal favorites). **Section 8.8 is literally titled "When to Invert"** — explicit technique recognition guidance. From the preface: worked examples explain "not only the solutions but also **how one would invent the solution to begin with**." Configuration recognition is framed as the core skill. 300+ practice problems, 248+ figures.

### AoPS Wiki: a reference encyclopedia, not a technique-recognition resource

**artofproblemsolving.com/wiki/** — The Theorems category contains exactly **97 pages** (Angle Bisector Theorem through Chicken McNugget Theorem to Vieta Jumping). Total technique-related articles likely **120–150+** including methods not categorized as theorems. Typical structure: definition/statement → proof(s) → practice problems (Introductory/Intermediate/Olympiad) → See Also links. **Recognition triggers are generally NOT included** — pages tell you WHAT a technique is and HOW it works but rarely WHEN to apply it. Many pages marked as stubs.

Notable exception: **Brilliant.org's wiki** sometimes fills the gap. Their Vieta Jumping page at **brilliant.org/wiki/vieta-root-jumping/** explicitly states: *"The tell-tale sign is the existence of a lemma giving a relationship between solutions. Look for a symmetric equation that has degree two in each variable."* This is the kind of recognition guidance AoPS wiki lacks.

### IMOMath: small archive, primarily a problem collection

**URL: imomath.com** — Companion site for "The IMO Compendium." Training materials organized by four topics (Algebra, Combinatorics, Numbers, Geometry) with roughly **10–15 technique articles** — a small collection. Articles (by Djukić, Matić, Novaković) read like concise textbook chapters with proofs and reasoning but **without competition-coaching "here's why I tried this approach" guidance**. Primary value is the IMO problem archive organized chronologically, not the training materials.

### Paul Zeitz's "Art and Craft of Problem Solving": the strategy-tactic-tool hierarchy

**3rd edition (2017), Wiley, 384 pages, ISBN 9781119239901.** The core innovation is a three-level hierarchy. **Strategies** (Chapter 2): high-level approaches — wishful thinking, penultimate step, getting your hands dirty, reformulating. **Tactics** (Chapter 3): mid-level tools with recognizable patterns — symmetry, extreme principle, pigeonhole, invariants, monotonization. **Crossover Tactics** (Chapter 4): graph theory, complex numbers, generating functions, mathematical games. **Subject Tools** (Chapters 5–9): algebra, combinatorics, number theory, geometry, calculus.

Zeitz provides **explicit recognition triggers within each chapter**: when you see a finite set, think about extreme elements; when you see a large set with limited categories, think pigeonhole; when you see a repeated transformation, look for invariants. The concept of "crux move" (from mountaineering) — identify the key obstacle first — and "penultimate step" — work backwards from solution — are unique pedagogical contributions. Used as a college textbook at USF. Rated 4.59/5 on Goodreads. **This is the best published resource for learning the strategy-selection layer of problem solving.**

### Engel's "Problem Solving Strategies": the closest to a technique pattern book

**Springer, 1998, 403 pages, 1,300+ problems, ISBN 978-0-387-98219-9.** Fourteen chapters in a hybrid organization: Chapters 1–4 by **strategy** (Invariance Principle, Coloring Proofs, Extremal Principle, Box/Pigeonhole Principle), Chapters 5–13 by **mathematical topic** (Enumerative Combinatorics, Number Theory, Inequalities, Induction, Sequences, Polynomials, Functional Equations, Geometry, Games), Chapter 14 Further Strategies. Each chapter: introduction → worked key examples (E1, E2...) → problem set with solutions.

**Engel provides explicit recognition triggers.** Chapter 1: *"If there is repetition, look for what does not change!"* Chapter 3: *"Pick the maximal element!"* Chapter 4: the difficulty is identifying what the "boxes" and "pearls" are. Chapter 5: *"If you cannot count the good objects, count the bad objects."* The strategy-first organization means reading Chapter 1 teaches you to **recognize invariance problems** across all mathematical domains. Notable gaps: no probability, no algorithmics, no advanced methods. Engel describes it as "probably the most complete training book on the market."

### Brilliant.org: interactive conceptual learning, not technique mapping

**URL: brilliant.org** — **90+ courses** across math, CS, data science, and science. Math covers foundations through calculus, number theory, discrete math, and statistics. Courses consist of interactive problem-solving exercises (~5–10 per section, ~15 minutes each) with custom intelligent feedback, visualizations, and animations. **No explicit technique recognition framework** — the pedagogical model is conceptual discovery through guided problem sequences, building intuition rather than mapping problem features to techniques. Pricing: ~$24.99/month. Good for learning concepts, not for competition technique selection.

### CP-Algorithms: encyclopedic competitive programming reference

**URL: cp-algorithms.com** — Free, volunteer-run English translation of Russian e-maxx.ru. **~120–130 articles** in 10 sections: Algebra (~28), Data Structures (~10), Dynamic Programming (~7), String Processing (~10), Linear Algebra (~4), Combinatorics (~7), Numerical Methods (~4), Geometry (~18), Graphs (~30+), Miscellaneous (~10). Full navigation at **cp-algorithms.com/navigation.html**. Each article: theory/motivation → mathematical derivation → C++ implementation → practice problem links.

The Algebra and Combinatorics sections overlap significantly with competition math (GCD, CRT, Euler's totient, Catalan numbers, inclusion-exclusion, FFT). But the focus is **algorithmic implementation** — how to code it efficiently — not proof-based problem solving. Useful as reference for computational competitions (IOI, Codeforces), less useful for proof-based olympiads. **No "when to use this technique" guidance.**

### The technique recognition gap: what doesn't exist

**Pólya's "How to Solve It"** (1945, Princeton, ~250 pages, 1M+ copies sold) provides the foundational 4-step method (Understand → Plan → Execute → Review) and a dictionary of heuristics. But it's deliberately general — "Have you seen this before?" rather than "if the problem has a symmetric degree-2 equation, try Vieta jumping." **Schoenfeld showed Pólya's heuristics are too broadly stated to be directly teachable**, which is why he developed the four-category framework emphasizing metacognitive control.

After exhaustive searching across published books, websites, forums, and academic literature: **no comprehensive, explicit "if you see feature X, try technique Y" decision tree for competition mathematics exists as a single published work.** The closest approximations are Engel's strategy-organized chapters (learn recognition through immersion), Chen's walkthroughs (showing the discovery metacognition explicitly), Zeitz's strategy-tactic-tool hierarchy (the conceptual framework for technique selection), and Gowers' frozen Tricki (the right structure with insufficient content). Scattered blog posts and AoPS forum discussions contain partial "if you see X, try Y" advice, but nobody has compiled these into a systematic mapping.

## Conclusion: the database that should exist but doesn't

The infrastructure for a "pattern database for problem solving" has been fully built in chess (ECO + Syzygy + CT-ART = openings + endgames + tactics), software engineering (GoF + EIP + PoEAA = design + integration + architecture patterns with explicit applicability metadata), and medicine (illness scripts + Isabel/DXplain/UpToDate = structured symptom-to-diagnosis retrieval). Cognitive science provides the theoretical justification: experts store ~50,000 domain-specific chunks that trigger associated actions (Chase & Simon), and what separates experts from knowledgeable novices is metacognitive control over when to deploy which strategy (Schoenfeld).

**In mathematics, the pieces exist but have never been assembled.** Engel and Zeitz provide strategy-level organization. Chen and Zhao provide technique-level exposition with occasional recognition guidance. AoPS Wiki provides 97+ theorem reference pages. Gowers' Tricki had the right architecture (problem-type → technique → "how to recognize when to use") but died from contributor friction. The CBR community built the retrieval architecture (PRODIGY/ANALOGY's annotated problem-solving traces with justifications at every choice point) but never applied it to mathematics. The gap is not in the individual components but in their integration: nobody has built the comprehensive problem-feature → technique → application-approach database that the user envisions, and the fact that Tricki — the most serious attempt — stalled after ~100–200 articles suggests the bottleneck is in the labor-intensive authoring of high-quality "when to recognize" guidance, not in the conceptual framework.
